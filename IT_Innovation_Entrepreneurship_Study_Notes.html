01 Recognising Business Opportunities

Deck: Recognizing Business Ideas — the difference between an idea and an opportunity, the three approaches to spotting opportunities, and the personal traits that help entrepreneurs see them first.

Definition
An opportunity is a favourable set of circumstances that creates a need for a new product, service, or business. It is not the same as an idea — an idea only becomes an opportunity when it is desirable, feasible, and viable enough for someone to act on.

Three ways to identify an opportunity

ApproachHow it worksExample
1. Observing trendsTrack economic, social, technological, and political/regulatory shifts for gaps they create.M-Pesa/M-Kopa "pay-as-you-go" models emerging from mobile + economic trends.
2. Solving a problemNotice a problem — often one you've personally experienced — and design a solution to it.Wind-farm start-ups solving the "alternative to fossil fuels" problem.
3. Finding gaps in the marketplaceSpot an underserved niche too small for mainstream retailers/manufacturers to bother with.Daisy Rock Guitars — guitars built specifically for women and girls.

The four trend categories (Approach 1, in detail)

Economic forces

A weak economy favours start-ups that help consumers save money (discount/"lipa later" models).

Social forces

Aging populations, workplace diversity, social-media use, mobile adoption, health & wellness focus.

Technological advances

New technology categories spawn entire industries (computing, internet, biotech, digital photography).

Political & regulatory change

New laws create compliance-driven business opportunities (e.g. environmental regulation → green business).

Personal characteristics that improve opportunity recognition

  • Prior industry experience — helps spot underserved niches and builds a network of useful contacts.
  • Cognitive factors ("entrepreneurial alertness") — a "sixth sense" for noticing opportunities without deliberately searching for them.
  • Social networks — weak-tie relationships (casual acquaintances) generate more new ideas than strong-tie relationships (close friends/coworkers), because weak ties introduce genuinely new information rather than reinforcing what you already believe. 40–50% of founders report getting their idea from a social contact.
  • Creativity — a five-stage process for generating novel, useful ideas.

Techniques for generating ideas

Brainstorming (no criticism, freewheeling, move fast, leap-frog on others' ideas) Focus groups (5–10 people, trained moderator) Library & internet research (BizMiner, ProQuest, IBISWorld, Mintel, LexisNexis) Customer advisory boards Day-in-the-life research
Exam angle
"Describe personal attributes of an entrepreneur from [a case]" questions want you to pick 3–4 of: prior experience, alertness/cognitive factors, social network strength, creativity — and tie each to a specific behaviour in the case, not just list the term.

02 Entrepreneurship Mindset

Deck: Entrepreneurship Mindset — definitions, characteristics of entrepreneurs, the four mindset domains, and how mindset is developed through Discover → Design → Prototype.

Definition
An entrepreneurial mindset is a set of beliefs, knowledge, and thought processes that equips people to recognise opportunities, take initiative, and succeed in diverse settings — the belief that a positive attitude and intentional behaviour can create solutions to problems of any size.

Characteristics of entrepreneurs (with examples)

TraitExample given
InnovationElon Musk
Risk-takingJeff Bezos (Amazon)
ResilienceJack Ma (Alibaba, 1999)
VisionSteve Jobs (Apple)
AdaptabilityReed Hastings (Netflix)
PassionSara Blakely
Customer focusStarbucks — "third place" positioning
PersistenceWalt Disney
NetworkingRichard Branson
LeadershipIndra Nooyi (PepsiCo)

Also generically: high need for achievement, not easily discouraged, comfort with financial uncertainty, open-minded to criticism, flexible when the data says pivot.

The four entrepreneurship mindset domains

Initiative & self-reliance

Owning a project without guidance; internal (not external) locus of control; perseveres until done.

Future orientation

Optimistic, goal-oriented, forward-thinking — prioritises long-term success over short-term comfort.

Opportunity recognition

Seeing problems as opportunities; tracking customer pain points and economic/tech/political trends; spotting market gaps.

Creativity & innovation

Thinking outside the box; idea maps; brainstorming with diverse groups; avoiding rigid structures and premature criticism.

Two further domains appear alongside these: critical thinking & problem-solving (e.g. the "5 Whys" technique) and communication & collaboration (e.g. Uber × Spotify's in-ride playlist partnership) and comfort with risk and flexibility & adaptability (optimism, quick pivoting, learning from setbacks).

Developing an entrepreneurial mindset (design-thinking approach)

Discover→ Design→ Prototype
Discover: market research & user interviews to find unmet needs · Design: generate and test solutions from empathy research · Prototype: a simple testable version used to validate the business hypothesis
Exam angle
"Personal traits of entrepreneurs" answers score better when you name the trait and give the matching real-world figure/example from the table above, rather than a bare list.

03 Entrepreneurship Mindset Skills

Deck: Entrepreneurship Mindset Skills — four skill categories entrepreneurs need, plus the Growth vs. Fixed Mindset distinction (Carol Dweck).

Four skill categories

CategorySkillsWhy it matters
1. Cognitive (thinking like an entrepreneur)Spotting gaps/unmet needs, critical thinking & problem-solving, creativity & innovation, financial literacyEntrepreneurs dissect complex problems and innovate solutions; understand budgeting, pricing, margins.
2. Behavioural (acting like an entrepreneur)Resilience & grit, risk-taking & decision-making, self-directed learning, resourcefulnessPersisting through setbacks; weighing pros/cons and acting under uncertainty; doing more with less.
3. Relational (leading like an entrepreneur)Communication & persuasion, collaboration & teamwork, networking, empathy & customer-centricityPitching ideas, leading diverse teams, leveraging connections, understanding audience needs.
4. Mindset foundationsGrowth mindset, ownership & accountability, ethical judgment & social responsibilityReframes failure as learning; balances profit with purpose.

Illustrative examples used: SCAMPER workshops and "Crazy 8s" for creativity; James Dyson's 5,126 failed prototypes for resilience; the Marshmallow Challenge for risk-taking/decision-making; "Constraints Challenges" (launch a campaign with $0) for resourcefulness.

Growth mindset vs. fixed mindset

Fixed mindset

Abilities are static; avoids challenges; gives up easily when things get hard.

Growth mindset

Abilities develop through dedication, effort, and learning; embraces challenges; treats failure as feedback.

Benefits of a growth mindset: increased resilience, greater perseverance, a lasting love of learning (Dweck's research links it to higher academic achievement).

04 Corporate & Social Entrepreneurship

Deck: Introduction to Entrepreneurship — Corporate & Social Entrepreneurship.

The "embattled corporation"

Four sources of turbulence force established firms to adopt new management practices:

Customers — fragmented markets, rising expectations Competitors — narrow, profitable niches, harder to differentiate Technology — new ways to manage info, production, logistics, sales Legal/regulatory/ethical — more accountability & litigation risk
Definition
Corporate entrepreneurship (intrapreneurship) is the development of new business ideas and opportunities within large, established corporations — as opposed to independent entrepreneurship, where individuals act on their own to create a new organisation.

The corporate entrepreneurship process

Innovation+ Corporate Venturing+ Strategic Renewal
= Corporate Entrepreneurship
  • Corporate venturing — managing new entrepreneurial businesses separately from the mainstream; investing in strategically important smaller firms.
  • Strategic renewal — sustained regeneration, domain redefinition, organisational rejuvenation, business model reconstruction.

Measures to build an intrapreneurial culture

  • Set explicit goals; give feedback and positive reinforcement
  • Emphasise individual responsibility; reward based on results, not punish failure
  • Grant autonomy/work discretion and time to work on future projects
  • Bet on people, not just analysis; encourage (don't mandate) innovative activity
  • Keep divisions/teams small (3M rule); tolerate failure; stay close to the customer

Social entrepreneurship

Definition
Social entrepreneurship combines the entrepreneurial drive to build innovative ventures with a focus on positive social impact rather than pure profit maximisation.

Four elements (Jeffrey A. Robinson): social impact, social innovation, sustainability (financial viability), and measurement (ability to document impact).

Not social entrepreneurship: pure social service provision (e.g. a children's home) or pure social activism (e.g. Mother Teresa) — a social enterprise is distinctive because it blends a social mission with a market-driven, often revenue-generating model.

Bottom-of-the-pyramid (BOP) context: >90% of adults in India/Africa fall in the lowest wealth tier vs. ~20% in developed countries — a large underserved market social entrepreneurs increasingly target (e.g. dollar-menu pricing, "kadogo" sachet products).

05 Sources of Innovation (Peter Drucker)

Deck: Sources of Innovation by Peter Drucker — the seven sources entrepreneurs scan for innovation opportunities.

"The entrepreneur always searches for change, responds to it, and exploits it as an opportunity." — Peter Drucker
SourceWhat it isExample
1. The UnexpectedUnexpected failures or successes, and unforeseen external events (economic shocks, tech breakthroughs, political change)."New Coke" failure (1985) → reverting to the original recipe drove a sales uplift; Java Jacket (paper cup sleeve) born from a spilled coffee.
2. IncongruityA gap between what is and what is assumed/expected to be — often surfaced through customer complaints.FedEx capitalising on dissatisfaction with USPS; Southwest Airlines' no-frills model.
3. Process NeedsWeak spots in existing workflows/processes — task-focused, not situation-focused innovation.ATMs → mobile banking → agency banking; food delivery replacing "eating out."
4. Industry/Market Structure ChangesShifts in how an industry is organised create room for new entrants.—
5. DemographicsChanges in population size, age structure, employment, education, income.—
6. Changes in Perception, Mood & MeaningShifts in mood, not fact ("glass half full vs. half empty") change what customers want.—
7. New KnowledgeScientific and non-scientific breakthroughs — longest lead time, needs convergence of multiple knowledge streams.—

Kenyan examples cited: M-Pesa (process/industry-structure innovation in banking, 2007); Tap2Eat by Wawira Njiru (food-for-education innovation).

Note
Sources 1–3 (Unexpected, Incongruity, Process Needs) arise inside the enterprise/industry; sources 4–7 (Structure, Demographics, Perception, New Knowledge) arise outside it.

06 Disruptive Innovation Theory

Deck: Disruptive Innovation Theory — Clayton Christensen's framework (The Innovator's Dilemma, 1997).

Definition
A disruptive innovation initially serves a niche market with lower performance/quality than the mainstream, but improves over time until it displaces incumbent products or services. Disruptive innovations are typically cheaper, simpler, more convenient, or more accessible — appealing to customers who are over- or under-served by dominant players.

Two types of disruption

Low-end disruption

Targets underserved/overlooked segments with simpler, cheaper solutions, then moves upmarket. E.g. Netflix (DVD-by-mail → streaming).

New-market disruption

Creates an entirely new market by addressing previously unmet needs. E.g. Tesla popularising EVs.

Common characteristics

  • Targets low-end or new-market segments incumbents ignore
  • Offers a different value proposition (lower cost, more convenient, more customisable)
  • Starts with lower performance, then improves on dimensions customers care about
  • Leverages enabling technologies/business models/platforms for competitive edge
  • Faces less resistance because incumbents are slow or unwilling to respond

The Innovator's Dilemma

Established firms must choose between sustaining innovation (improving existing products) and pursuing disruptive opportunities. Incumbents often fail to respond because they prioritise short-term gains — e.g. Kodak's reluctance to embrace digital photography.

ExampleDisrupted
Personal computersMainframes / minicomputers
Digital camerasFilm photography
NetflixVideo rental & cable TV
UberTaxis & car rental
AirbnbHotels
M-PesaTraditional banking (Kenya, 2007)
AWSOn-premise IT infrastructure (started as cloud infra, now dominates)

Applying DIT in entrepreneurship

  1. Identify low-end/underserved consumer markets with unmet or latent needs
  2. Design offerings that appeal emotionally as well as functionally (e.g. Airbnb's "local, unique stays" vs. standardised hotels)
  3. Seek continuous customer feedback and monitor market trends to anticipate future needs

07 Customer Discovery Process

Deck: Customer Discovery — validating that a real problem (and real customers for the solution) exists before building.

Definition
Customer discovery is the process of validating the problem a product/service aims to solve — a "reality check" against pure concept testing. Investors see it as due diligence; design thinkers see it as an empathetic, human-centred process; marketers see it as the foundation for messaging that resonates.

The 8-step process

1. Identify segments→ 2. Form hypotheses→ 3. Interviews & surveys→ 4. Observe behaviour
5. Iterate on feedback→ 6. Build MVP→ 7. Test MVP→ 8. Analyse & refine

Worked example: a food-delivery startup assumes speed is the top priority. Interviews reveal customers actually care most about food quality and temperature on arrival — the insight causes a pivot from "speed" to "quality during delivery," reshaping the whole business model.

Exam angle
Customer discovery is not a one-off event — it continues after launch as a way of continuously adapting to evolving customer needs. This "ongoing, not one-time" framing is a common exam distinction.

08 The Lean Startup Methodology Most exam-tested

Deck: The Lean Start-Up Innovation Methodology — Eric Ries's approach to building startups under extreme uncertainty.

Worked example: Zappos

Nick Swinmurn wasn't sure people would buy shoes online. Instead of building warehouses, he photographed shoes from local stores, posted them online, and bought/shipped from the store only after a real order came in — testing the core assumption "customers are willing to buy shoes online" with almost no investment.

Validated learning
Learning is validated when it comes from real customer behaviour (e.g. actual purchases), not from opinions or surveys. It's a practical way to reduce risk and confirm whether an idea solves a genuine problem before scaling investment.

The Build–Measure–Learn loop

Build→ Measure→ Learn↻ back to Build
The startup cycles through this loop repeatedly, using each round of learning to decide whether to persevere or pivot.

Benefits of building an MVP (Minimum Viable Product)

  • Quickly validate the idea — test assumptions, get real customer feedback
  • Minimise risk — test with minimal time/resource investment
  • Improve product development — build only the core features customers actually need, reducing waste
  • Gain customer insight — learn real needs and preferences
  • Speed to market — get the product to real users faster
  • Attract investors — demonstrates viability and traction

Key metrics to validate a startup hypothesis

MetricWhat it measures
User AcquisitionNumber of new users signing up
User Retention% of users who keep using the product over time
EngagementHow actively users interact with the product
Conversion Rate% of users taking a specific action (purchase, sign-up)
Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)Total value a customer generates over the relationship
Net Promoter Score (NPS)Customer satisfaction & loyalty ("would you recommend us?")
Cost per Acquisition (CPA/CAC)Cost of acquiring one new customer

Real companies built the lean-startup way

Dropbox

MVP = a simple demo video. Thousands signed up before any code was written.

Airbnb

Founders rented out their own apartment to test if strangers would pay to stay with a stranger.

Zappos

Sold shoe photos before owning any inventory (see above).

Spotify

Launched a basic streaming service and iterated continuously on user feedback.

Instagram

Pivoted from a broader app to focus purely on core photo-sharing.

Slack

Tested the internal comms tool with small teams before scaling to enterprise.

Exam angle
Related concepts frequently tested alongside the Lean Startup: engines of growth (viral, sticky, paid), CAC vs. CLV, beachhead market selection, market sizing (top-down vs. bottom-up), and Bill Gross's factors for startup success (timing, team/execution, idea, business model, funding). None of these are on their own separate deck — they attach to the lean-startup / business-model-testing unit.

09 Business Model & Value Proposition Canvas

Deck: Business Model and Value Canvas.

Definition
A business model is the framework describing how a business creates, delivers, and captures value — covering products/services, target customers, revenue streams, cost structure, and key partnerships.

Business Model Canvas — 9 building blocks

BlockKey question
Customer SegmentsFor whom are we creating value? Who are our target/most important customers?
Value PropositionWhat value do we deliver? Which customer problem are we solving?
ChannelsWhich channels do our customer segments want to be reached through?
Customer RelationshipsWhat relationship does each segment expect us to establish?
Revenue StreamsWhat are customers really willing to pay for?
Key ResourcesWhat resources do our value proposition, channels, and relationships require?
Key ActivitiesWhat key activities does our value proposition require?
Key PartnersWho are our key partners/suppliers? What do we get from them?
Cost StructureWhat are the most important/expensive costs in our model?

Why a business model matters

  • Value creation — clarifies what makes the offer different from competitors
  • Revenue generation — defines pricing, channels, and customer segments that produce income
  • Cost structure — surfaces fixed/variable costs and areas for optimisation
  • Competitive advantage — an innovative model differentiates and enables scalability

Business model innovation

Fresh approaches to serving customers and generating profit — focused on overall strategy, not just product features. Examples: Netflix (DVD-rental → subscription streaming), Airbnb (peer-to-peer home-sharing), Tesla (direct-to-consumer EV sales).

Key factors for success: awareness of customer demands, willingness to experiment/take calculated risks, a strong innovation culture, and the ability to communicate and implement change across the company.

Value Proposition Canvas

Customer Profile

Jobs — what the customer is trying to get done (functional, social, emotional). Pains — the roadblocks, risks, and negative outcomes they want to avoid. Gains — the outcomes and benefits they want.

Value Map

Products & Services that support each job. Pain Relievers — how the offer eases specific pains. Gain Creators — how it produces the gains customers want.

Fit is achieved when the Value Map's pain relievers and gain creators clearly match the Customer Profile's most important pains, gains, and jobs.

How to fill it in (sequence)

  1. Choose a customer segment
  2. List and prioritise their jobs
  3. List and prioritise their pains
  4. List and prioritise their gains
  5. Pick the top 3–5 pains/gains tied to the most important jobs
  6. List all product/service benefits, pain relievers, and gain creators
  7. Pick the 3–5 that make the biggest difference
  8. Link relievers/creators back to the specific pains/gains/jobs they solve
  9. Define your edge over competitors; write clear, jargon-free value propositions; test them

When to use the Value Proposition Canvas

  • Developing a new feature for an existing product
  • Expanding into a new market
  • Checking product-market fit — alignment between the Customer Profile and the Value Map signals fit and raises the odds of success

Types of business models

TypeDescriptionExamples
Product-basedSells tangible productsManufacturing, retail, e-commerce
Service-basedSells intangible servicesConsulting, healthcare, financial services
Subscription-basedRecurring revenue for ongoing accessStreaming platforms, SaaS
Platform-basedConnects two or more user groups (multi-sided)M-Pesa, Uber, Amazon Marketplace, Airbnb
FranchiseIndependent operators use an established brand/model for fees—
FreemiumFree basic tier, paid premium featuresZoom, Spotify, Dropbox
LicensingGrants rights to use IP/tech for a feeUber licensing Google Maps
Freemium — pros & cons
Pros: easy user acquisition and data collection, wider reach as a marketing tool, ad revenue, strong brand awareness with low support cost.
Cons: free users often never convert; too many free features removes the incentive to upgrade; users may tire of a limited free tier.

Types of revenue models

Ad-based · Affiliate · Transactional (direct/web) · Subscription · Channel/indirect sales · Commission marketplace · Licensing · Retail

10 Lean Canvas Tool

Deck: Lean Canvas Tool Explained — a leaner, startup-focused alternative to the Business Model Canvas.

Definition
The Lean Canvas (developed by Ash Maurya) is a business-modelling tool that deconstructs a startup idea into its key and riskiest assumptions — a tactical plan for navigating from idea to a working business.

Why use it

  • Puts ideas on paper and creates clarity of business goals
  • Translates thoughts into words visually, faster than a full business plan
  • Helps entrepreneurs test their hypotheses efficiently

The 9 blocks of the Lean Canvas

BlockKey question
ProblemWhat problem does your product solve? How common is it? What would people pay to fix it?
SolutionTop 3 features that solve the problem. Are there similar solutions already?
Key MetricsWhat indicators show your product/business is working (engagement, revenue, satisfaction)?
Unique Value PropositionSits at the intersection of Problem + Solution — what makes you different and worth attention?
Unfair AdvantageWhat can't easily be copied? (IP — patents, copyrights, trademarks; unique product features)
ChannelsRoute to market — marketing, communication, distribution (blogs, social media, traditional media).
Customer SegmentsWho are your prospects, and how do they relate to the problem?
Cost StructureOperational costs, customer acquisition cost, distribution cost, supplier/partner costs.
Revenue StreamsRevenue model, gross margins, and lifetime value — not just initial but recurring revenue.
Exam angle
"Complete the lean canvas — one item per block" questions want exactly one crisp entry per block above, pulled straight from the case study text.

11 Business Idea Feasibility Analysis

Deck: Business Idea Feasibility Analysis.

Definition
Feasibility analysis is the preliminary evaluation of whether a business idea is viable and worth pursuing — conducted early, before major resources are committed, to screen ideas before spending on them.

The four forms of feasibility analysis

FormPurposeSub-components
Product/Service FeasibilityAssesses the overall appeal of the proposed product/service.Desirability (does it make sense? any fatal flaws? use a concept statement/test) and Demand (talk face-to-face with potential customers; use online tools like Google AdWords/landing pages, surveys, Q&A sites).
Industry/Target Market FeasibilityAssesses the appeal of the industry and the specific target market within it.Industry attractiveness (are trends moving for or against it?) and Target market attractiveness (big enough to matter, small enough to avoid big competitors).
Organizational FeasibilityDetermines if the business has sufficient (non-financial) management expertise, competence, and resources to launch.Management prowess (passion + market understanding of the founding team) and Resource sufficiency (6–12 most critical non-financial resources: space, contract manufacturers, key staff, IP protection, partnerships).
Financial FeasibilityFinal component — a preliminary financial assessment is enough at this stage.Total start-up cash needed, financial performance of similar businesses (industry reports or observational research), and overall financial attractiveness (growth rate, recurring revenue %, forecastability, internally generated funds, investor exit options).
Exam angle
"Key elements to consider in feasibility analysis" essentially wants all four forms named with one sub-point each — Product/Service, Industry/Market, Organizational, Financial — applied to the specific case (e.g. cashless payment systems, ride-hailing).
