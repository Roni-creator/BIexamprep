01 — Recognising Business Opportunities

Describe personal attributes of an entrepreneur from Sarah, the founder of Organic Delights.
BBIT4102 Q1(a) · 8 marks
  1. Passion/personal conviction — Sarah's "fervent advocacy for healthy eating" is the personal belief that drove her to start the business and sustains her through challenges.
  2. Customer focus — sharing educational content on nutrition and recipes shows she prioritises customer needs over just selling products.
  3. Networking/relationship-building — partnering with local farmers and collaborating with influencers and health experts shows an ability to build the relationships a growing business needs.
  4. Vision/future orientation — considering physical stores while still scaling online shows she can see beyond the current model toward future growth.
Describe four personal traits of entrepreneurs which make them better at recognizing business opportunities.
BBT4202 Q2(d) · 4 marks
  1. Prior industry experience — helps spot underserved niches and builds a network of useful contacts.
  2. Cognitive alertness ("entrepreneurial alertness") — a "sixth sense" for noticing opportunities without deliberately searching.
  3. Strong social networks, especially weak ties — casual acquaintances introduce genuinely new information more often than close friends/coworkers do.
  4. Creativity — the ability to generate novel, useful ideas through a structured creative process.
Explain approaches used by entrepreneurs to identify business opportunities.
BBT4202 Q2(b) · 6 marks
  1. Observing trends — tracking economic, social, technological, and political/regulatory shifts for the gaps they create.
  2. Solving a problem — noticing a problem, often personally experienced, and designing a solution to it.
  3. Finding gaps in the marketplace — spotting an underserved niche too small for mainstream players to bother with.
Discuss three current trends responsible for the growth of businesses such as Organic Delights.
BBIT4102 Q1(d) · 6 marks
  1. Social/lifestyle trend — rising health and wellness consciousness pushing demand toward organic, "clean" food.
  2. Demographic/economic trend — a growing urban middle class with more disposable income willing to pay a premium for perceived healthier food.
  3. Technological trend — e-commerce and social media enabling nationwide reach and cheap distribution of educational content.

05 — Sources of Innovation / Trends

Explain two of the four primary drivers of technology ventures applicable to the Quickcook case.
BBIT4202 Q1(a) · 4 marks
  1. Technological change — Quickcook runs its ordering and payment entirely on existing mobile infrastructure (WhatsApp and M-Pesa), keeping the operation lean.
  2. Knowledge/information-driven economy — the startup treats customer feedback data (gathered through daily follow-up calls) as a core input for continuously refining its offering.
Explain three socio-cultural changes that create new entrepreneurial opportunities.
BBT4202 Q4(b) · 6 marks
  1. Growing health and wellness consciousness, opening markets for related products and services.
  2. Increasing urbanisation and changing lifestyles, shifting demand toward convenience-driven services.
  3. Rising mobile technology and social media use, changing how people discover, buy from, and interact with businesses.
Describe the type of technology used in the innovation of intelligent gloves.
BBIT4102 Q3(a) · 10 marks
  1. Wearable technology — the glove is worn on the body.
  2. Sensor technology — captures finger gestures and articulations, converting them into electrical signals.
  3. Embedded/microelectronics — a coin-sized circuit board processes and transmits the signal.
  4. Wireless communication technology (e.g. Bluetooth) — sends the signal from the wrist board to a smartphone.
  5. Speech synthesis/text-to-speech software — converts the transmitted impulses into spoken words at roughly one word per second, together making this an Internet of Things (IoT) innovation.

07 — Customer Discovery

Explain why understanding target customers is key to innovation.
BBIT4102 Q5(a) · 8 marks
  1. Ensures the product solves a real, strongly felt problem — here, that helmets were uncomfortable and unfashionable, not that helmets were unwanted.
  2. Reduces the risk of building something nobody actually wants, however technically impressive.
  3. Improves product-market fit by aligning design with what customers actually value.
  4. Sharpens the value proposition and marketing message.
  5. Supports ongoing adaptation, since understanding customers is never a one-off task.
Describe three methods the team can use to obtain feedback from users.
BBIT4102 Q5(b) · 6 marks
  1. Customer interviews/surveys — directly asking cyclists why they avoid helmets.
  2. Focus groups — gathering a small group to discuss comfort, appearance, and safety.
  3. Observing behaviour — watching actual cycling/helmet-wearing habits, since stated preference and real behaviour can differ.
Explain benefits of conducting user testing before launching the platform.
BBIT4102 Q2(b) · 6 marks
  1. Surfaces usability problems before they affect a wide audience.
  2. Validates product-market fit — confirms the platform solves the problem it claims to.
  3. Generates actionable feedback to refine features before a costly public launch.
  4. Builds an early-adopter base and word-of-mouth ahead of the official launch.
  5. Reduces the risk of a damaging public failure.
Advise on five key metrics useful in validating the Mkulima Bora app.
BBT4202 Q3(b) · 10 marks
  1. User acquisition — new farmers registering (currently ~2,000).
  2. Active usage/retention — daily active users (~200/day) relative to the registered base.
  3. Engagement — social media following and interaction (13,000 users/clients).
  4. Conversion rate — proportion of registered farmers converting to paying subscribers.
  5. Revenue metrics — actual subscription revenue collected against targets.

08 — The Lean Startup Methodology Heaviest weighting

Explain the business model testing process.
BBIT4202 Q1(b) · 8 marks
  1. Formulate hypotheses about the riskiest assumptions (e.g. "vendors will pay for pre-boiled pulses").
  2. Design a small, low-cost experiment or MVP rather than building the full business.
  3. Run the test with real customers — as Quickcook did with vendors in different urban centres.
  4. Collect data on actual behaviour (orders placed, repeat purchases, attrition).
  5. Analyse the data to validate or invalidate the hypothesis.
  6. Decide to persevere or pivot.
  7. Scale only once the model is validated with real evidence.
Discuss three of Bill Gross's elements that might apply to Quickcook's success.
BBIT4202 Q1(e) · 6 marks
  1. Timing — launching when 83% of the working population already buys meals from food vendors is strong market timing.
  2. Team/execution — using interns and recent graduates to move quickly through testing shows fast, lean execution.
  3. Business model — buying pulses at bulk discount and passing on savings while keeping a margin gives a clear path to sustainability.
Identify three ways Quickcook can use past customers to drive sustainable growth.
BBIT4202 Q1(d) · 3 marks
  1. Referrals from satisfied vendors to others in their network.
  2. Testimonials/case studies used in marketing to new prospects.
  3. Upselling additional products to the existing vendor base.
Explain the three engines of growth.
BBIT4202 Q2(a) · 6 marks
  1. Sticky engine — growth through retention; keeping existing customers engaged so fewer leave than join.
  2. Viral engine — growth spreads through customers themselves via referrals/network effects.
  3. Paid engine — growth bought through paid marketing, sustainable only while customer lifetime value exceeds acquisition cost.
Discuss three ways a startup might reduce customer acquisition costs.
BBIT4202 Q2(b) · 6 marks
  1. Lean on referral/viral growth instead of purely paid channels.
  2. Improve retention so CAC is recovered over a longer customer lifetime.
  3. Use targeted, data-driven marketing to cut wasted ad spend, or invest in content marketing/SEO.
Identify three practical applications of Customer Lifetime Value.
BBIT4202 Q2(c) · 3 marks
  1. Sets a ceiling on acceptable CAC (CAC should stay well below CLV).
  2. Helps prioritise which customer segments to target.
  3. Informs how much to invest in retention/loyalty programs.
Identify three criteria a startup can use to identify its beachhead market.
BBIT4202 Q3(c) · 3 marks
  1. Large enough to be viable, small enough to dominate quickly.
  2. Customers share an urgent, well-defined need.
  3. Reachable through a clear channel, and can serve as a reference base for expansion.
Explain two reasons market size calculation matters to investors and entrepreneurs.
BBIT4202 Q5(b) · 4 marks
  1. Helps assess the scalability and return potential of the opportunity.
  2. Helps the entrepreneur allocate resources and prioritise the largest addressable segments.
What three limitations does the bottom-up approach to market sizing have?
BBIT4202 Q5(c) · 3 marks
  1. Time-consuming and resource-intensive to gather granular data.
  2. May underestimate the true market, relying only on directly observable data.
  3. Harder to project future growth, since it's built from current, static figures.
Discuss five benefits of developing a prototype and testing it in the product development process.
BBT4202 Q5(a) · 10 marks
  1. Quickly validates the idea — tests assumptions with real customer feedback.
  2. Minimises risk — tests with minimal time/resource investment.
  3. Improves product development — builds only the features customers actually need.
  4. Generates customer insight — deepens understanding of real needs.
  5. Helps attract investors — demonstrates viability and traction.
Describe five approaches commonly used in testing a prototype.
BBT4202 Q5(b) · 10 marks
  1. Usability testing — observing real users interact with the prototype.
  2. A/B testing — comparing variants against a metric.
  3. Beta testing — releasing to a limited target-user group under real conditions.
  4. Customer interviews/feedback sessions after hands-on use.
  5. Concept/landing-page testing — measuring signups or expressed interest before the product is fully built.

Explain four elements of a compelling value proposition.
BBIT4102 Q1(b) · 8 marks
  1. Clarity of value delivered — organic, natural products for health-conscious customers.
  2. Differentiation from competitors — a "one-stop-shop" for organic/gluten-free/vegan products.
  3. Relevance to customer pain points — educational content eases not knowing how to eat healthily.
  4. Credibility/trust signals — partnerships with farmers and health experts.
Demonstrate how entrepreneurs can use the value proposition canvas to create better product-service fit.
BBIT4202 Q5(a) · 8 marks
  1. Choose a specific customer segment.
  2. Map and prioritise their jobs (functional, social, emotional).
  3. Map and prioritise their pains.
  4. Map and prioritise their gains.
  5. Design pain relievers and gain creators that directly address the top-prioritised pains and gains.
  6. Check for fit — the value map should visibly match the customer profile.
  7. Test the resulting value proposition with real customers before committing further resources.
Identify and explain five elements of the business model canvas in the Uber case.
BBT4202 Q1(a) · 10 marks
  1. Customer segments — riders needing transport, and drivers seeking flexible income.
  2. Value proposition — convenient, on-demand rides with automatic fare calculation and nearest-driver matching.
  3. Channels — the mobile app, the sole interface connecting both sides.
  4. Revenue streams — a commission taken from each completed trip.
  5. Key partners/resources — drivers supplying their own vehicles, and the matching/GPS technology platform.
Explain what a comprehensive "customer experience" innovation would involve.
BBIT4202 Q3(a) · 8 marks
  1. Service — support and delivery that actively enhance the core offering, not just accompany it.
  2. Channel — rethinking how and where customers access the business.
  3. Brand — how the company is presented and perceived, building emotional connection.
  4. Customer engagement — distinctive, memorable interactions (community, personalisation, gamification) that differentiate on how it feels to be a customer.

11 — Business Idea Feasibility Analysis

Advise the team on key elements to consider when conducting a business idea feasibility analysis.
BBIT4102 Q4(a) · 10 marks
  1. Product/service feasibility — is a cashless smart-card fare system desirable to commuters, and is there real demand (test via commuter interviews or a small pilot)?
  2. Industry/target market feasibility — how attractive is the public transport payments space right now, and how large/reachable is the target market of daily commuters?
  3. Organizational feasibility — does the team have the expertise and partnerships (SACCOs, bus operators, hardware suppliers) to launch and operate the system?
  4. Financial feasibility — what is the total start-up cash needed for card readers and infrastructure, and is the revenue model financially viable?
Explain how Uber's feasibility analysis could have helped address potential challenges in various markets.
BBT4202 Q1(b) · 12 marks
  1. Product/service feasibility — testing early whether smartphone users in a given city would trust and adopt app-based ride requests before committing to a full build.
  2. Industry/target market feasibility — assessing, market by market, how attractive the local taxi/transport industry is and how regulation is trending before expanding — this could have anticipated regulatory pushback and taxi-union resistance.
  3. Organizational feasibility — evaluating whether the local team has the expertise and relationships to navigate each new regulatory environment before launch.
  4. Financial feasibility — estimating start-up capital needed per market and planning funding rounds accordingly, avoiding undercapitalised expansion.

Applied well, this would have surfaced regulatory risk, driver-supply constraints, and local competitive intensity before costly market entry rather than reactively.

04 — Corporate & Social Entrepreneurship

Explain four measures that should be undertaken by an organization wishing to establish an intrapreneurial culture.
BBT4202 Q2(a) · 4 marks
  1. Set explicit goals for innovation activity, with feedback and positive reinforcement.
  2. Reward based on results, not punish failure.
  3. Grant autonomy/work discretion and time to pursue new ideas.
  4. Keep divisions/teams small and stay close to the customer (the "3M rule").

Market fundamentalism & technological solutionism; reasons for ethical lapses; holistic ethical management.
BBIT4202 Q4(a)(b)(c) · 15 marks
  • Market fundamentalism: the belief that market forces alone will self-correct ethical problems, minimising the perceived need for regulation.
  • Technological solutionism: the belief that technology alone can resolve social/ethical problems without addressing root causes — often used to justify a product despite legitimate concerns.
  • Tension: internal company logic pushes toward growth (market fundamentalism), while external stakeholders demand accountability; solutionism is the rhetorical bridge companies use between the two.
  • Reasons for ethical lapses: growth pressure/"move fast" culture; weak governance structures in early-stage firms; leadership blind spots from a lack of diverse perspectives.
  • Holistic ethical management: a clear code of ethics embedded in culture; accountability mechanisms (ethics review, whistleblower channels); ongoing stakeholder engagement and transparency.
